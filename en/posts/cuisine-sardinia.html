Sardinian Cuisine: Dishes, Wine & Restaurant Tips

Sardinian cuisine differs noticeably from the rest of Italy: shaped by shepherd and fishing traditions, with its own pasta shapes, its own bread, and its own wines. An overview of what you should try.

Classics you shouldn't miss on any trip

  • Culurgiones – stuffed pasta parcels, usually filled with potato, mint, and pecorino, traditionally sealed with an elaborate braided edge.
  • Fregola – small, rounded pasta pearls, often prepared with clams and saffron.
  • Porceddu – suckling pig roasted on a spit, a festive dish you can try at traditional countryside restaurants (agriturismi).
  • Pane Carasau – wafer-thin, crispy flatbread, originally created as long-lasting provisions for shepherds.
  • Bottarga – smoked, pressed fish roe (usually grey mullet), finely grated over pasta.
  • Seadas – fried pastry parcels filled with pecorino, served warm with honey.

Cheese and wine

Pecorino Sardo is the island's best-known cheese, ranging from mild to sharp and spicy. Among the wines, it's especially worth trying the Vermentino di Gallura (a fresh white wine from the northeast) and the robust red Cannonau – rich in polyphenols and often mentioned in connection with the exceptional longevity of the population in the mountainous Barbagia region, one of the world's recognized "Blue Zones."

A dish that belongs on every menu: Fregola with clams – Sardinia's answer to couscous.

How to spot a good restaurant

  • Short, changing menu: a sign of fresh, seasonal cooking rather than frozen food for tourists.
  • Agriturismi away from the coast: countryside restaurants inland often offer the most authentic and affordable food – frequently cash-only, but with real home cooking.
  • Busy places full of locals: a simple but reliable indicator away from the main promenade.
  • Be cautious of menus with photos and many languages right on the waterfront – usually a sign of a place geared toward passing tourist trade.
Practical tip: In peak season (July/August), it's worth reserving a table a day ahead in popular towns – outside the season this usually isn't necessary.

The Best Liqueurs of Sardinia

Few drinks represent Sardinia as much as a digestivo to close out a meal. Three names are worth remembering.

Mirto – the island's liqueur

Mirto is made from the berries or leaves of the myrtle plant that grows wild across Sardinia, and has been part of festive meals for generations. There are two versions: Mirto Rosso, the classic red liqueur made from the dark berries, with fruity, slightly bitter notes; and Mirto Bianco, a lighter, milder version made from young shoots and leaves. Mirto became commercially popular especially through the tourism boom on the Costa Smeralda starting in the 1970s.

Fil'e Ferru – the clear spirit

A traditional, strong grape spirit (comparable to grappa), whose name refers to the "iron wire" once used to mark buried barrels during the era of illegal home distilling. Noticeably sharper and less sweet than Mirto – more for fans of strong clear spirits.

Limoncino – the Sardinian lemon version

The Sardinian take on the well-known Limoncello, made from the peel of local lemons. Sweeter and lighter than Mirto or Fil'e Ferru, usually served well chilled.

A digestivo after the meal isn't the exception in Sardinia – it's the rule. Many restaurants and agriturismi bring it automatically along with the bill.

Practical notes

  • Known producers such as San Martino, Bresca Dorada, or Silvio Carta are a good indicator of quality if you're looking for a bottle to take home.
  • Homemade versions at small agriturismi or trattorias are often milder or more intense than the industrial version – a good reason to try it locally instead of blindly buying a brand.
  • As a gift: small 0.5-liter bottles are an easy option for your luggage.
Practical tip: Mirto tastes best well chilled, almost ice-cold – many places keep the bottle in the freezer for this reason.
